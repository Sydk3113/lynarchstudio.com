Common mistakes and better decisions

Many project difficulties begin as small decisions made without enough context.

Starting with a reference image instead of a brief

A beautiful room may have different dimensions, light or maintenance needs from yours. Use images to explain atmosphere and preferences, then test the design against your actual site and daily routines.

Choosing finishes before resolving the layout

Materials cannot correct awkward movement or missing storage. Check furniture, door swings, working clearances and service access before committing to decorative selections.

Comparing only the bottom line of quotations

A low total may omit fittings, installation, preparation or specific material standards. Compare item descriptions, quantities, exclusions and responsibilities before deciding.

Treating a render as the complete instruction

Visualisations show design intent but do not replace measured drawings, specifications or verified site dimensions. Confirm the information needed for execution before ordering or fabrication.

Leaving services until after ceilings and joinery

Lighting, air-conditioning, plumbing and electrical access compete for space. Coordinate them early, and check maintenance access before enclosing them.

Changing decisions without a written record

A verbal instruction can have consequences across several trades. Record the scope, price and timing impact of changes before authorising them. Ensure everyone uses the current drawing revision.

Assuming someone else is checking the site

Design reviews, contractor supervision and specialist inspections are different activities. Name the responsible person for each, agree the review stages and keep a record of unresolved issues.

Rushing handover

A finished appearance does not mean every system has been demonstrated or every document received. Walk through the work, record snags, gather the agreed manuals and warranties, and confirm follow-up responsibilities.

A useful habit: before any irreversible decision, ask what information supports it, who must review it and what else it affects.
